## Section: framing

### The stranger-danger model is wrong
- **key:** `stranger_danger_wrong`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

In the cases where children physically met someone they first met online, 100% went willingly. Predators do not present as strangers — they build familiarity and trust first, so by the time a meeting is proposed the child does not experience them as a stranger. Teaching "don't talk to strangers" does not address how this actually happens.

- **Source:** NCMEC (National Center for Missing & Exploited Children) — **`needs_source: true`**. Attributed to NCMEC per operator; the specific primary study and the exact figure must be confirmed by the reviewer before this is stated to a client as fact.

### Grooming can happen in under a week
- **key:** `grooming_can_be_fast`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

"Watch for a slow pattern" is wrong advice. CEOP has documented cases where the entire grooming cycle — first contact to attempted abuse — occurred in under a week. Do not assume you have weeks to notice a gradual change.

- **Source:** CEOP (UK Child Exploitation and Online Protection) — **`needs_source: true`**. Attributed to CEOP per operator; the specific primary report and the under-a-week claim must be confirmed by the reviewer before this is stated to a client as fact.

## Section: platform

### Roblox
- **key:** `roblox`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

**Risk profile:** Very large young user base; in-experience chat and private servers; the Robux economy is a common grooming and gift vector.

**Contact patterns to watch:**
- Adults posing as peers inside popular experiences
- Moving chat into private servers away from moderation
- Offering Robux or premium items as gifts

**Settings a parent can verify:**
- Turn Account Restrictions ON (limits to curated content and contacts)
- Set an Account PIN so the child cannot change safety settings
- Set chat and contact permissions by age — and verify the declared age is correct
- Review the child's friends list and any private-server access

### Minecraft
- **key:** `minecraft`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

**Risk profile:** Public multiplayer servers carry unmoderated chat and voice; private realms are controllable.

**Contact patterns to watch:**
- Contact on a public server then a push to Discord or DMs
- Befriending on a server then requesting private contact

**Settings a parent can verify:**
- Use a private invite-only Realm rather than public servers
- Restrict or disable multiplayer and chat in account/family settings
- Review who is on the Realm allow-list
- On console, set communication to Friends-only via the platform family settings

### Discord
- **key:** `discord`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

**Risk profile:** Direct messages and server joins are the primary risk; default DM filtering is weak and the adult population is large.

**Contact patterns to watch:**
- A DM after meeting on a game or server
- An invite to a private server
- Requests for images or a move to Snapchat/Telegram

**Settings a parent can verify:**
- Enable the "Keep me safe" DM content filter (scans all direct messages)
- Turn OFF "Allow direct messages from server members" by default
- Restrict who can add the child as a friend to Friends-of-Friends or none
- Review joined servers and remove unknown ones

### Snapchat
- **key:** `snapchat`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

**Risk profile:** Ephemeral content reduces evidence; Snap Map can share location; Quick Add surfaces strangers.

**Contact patterns to watch:**
- Location-based contact via Snap Map
- Quick Add suggestions connecting the child to strangers
- Requests for disappearing images (a common sextortion vector)

**Settings a parent can verify:**
- Enable Ghost Mode so location is NOT shared on Snap Map
- Set "Contact Me" and "See My Location" to Friends-only or Only Me
- Turn off Quick Add visibility
- Review the friends list for unknown accounts

## Section: cross_platform

### Cross-platform migration — the highest-value warning signal
- **key:** `migration_signal`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

Contact that starts in a moderated game (Roblox, Minecraft) and then moves to weaker-moderation private messaging (Discord DMs, Snapchat, Telegram, WhatsApp) is the single strongest warning sign. Moderation and evidence both drop at the moment of the move — which is exactly why a predator pushes for it. If a child mentions a new online friend who wants to talk somewhere else, treat it as significant.

### In-game currency is the gift signal
- **key:** `ingame_currency`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

Parents watch for physical gifts and miss that the transaction is digital. Robux, V-Bucks, gift cards, or a suddenly upgraded or premium account can be the grooming gift. A child receiving in-game currency or premium items from an online contact is the same warning as an adult buying a child physical presents.

### Age-accurate account settings
- **key:** `age_settings`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

Platforms apply protections based on the age an account declared at signup. A child who signed up as 18 has silently disabled every age-based safeguard — content filters, contact restrictions, and default privacy. Verify the declared age on each account matches the child's real age; if it does not, the protections you assume are on are off.

## Section: protocol

### Warning signs — sustained change with secrecy, not single bad days
- **key:** `warning_signs`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

The reliable signal is a sustained change over weeks paired with secrecy or distress around devices — hiding the screen, anxiety when a device is taken away, a new online friend they will not discuss, staying up to be online. A single bad day, ordinary teenage moodiness, or simply wanting more privacy are NOT warning signs; confusing normal development with danger leads a parent to either miss the real signal or panic at nothing. Because grooming can also be fast, a sudden and intense new secrecy is worth attention too — the pattern is secrecy plus change, not duration alone. Risk is not uniform by age — research places the highest risk roughly between ages 12 and 15, so calibrate attention accordingly, while knowing younger and older children are not immune.

### Do not confront the predator
- **key:** `no_confront`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

A parent's instinct is to message or confront the account. Do not. It tips the predator off — they delete the account and the evidence, block, and the case dies — and it can escalate risk to the child. Preserve evidence first, involve the platform and authorities, and never contact the account yourself.

### Preserve evidence before any action
- **key:** `evidence_first`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

Before confronting your child, changing settings, or reporting: document and preserve. Screenshot the conversations, profiles, usernames, and any images or requests; record dates, times, and the platform. Do not delete anything. Evidence preserved now is what lets authorities and platforms act — evidence lost cannot be recovered.

### Sextortion — emergency protocol
- **key:** `sextortion`
- **is_emergency:** true
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

Sextortion is distinct from grooming, time-critical, and the correct response is counterintuitive. A perpetrator — often posing as a peer — obtains an intimate image and then threatens to share it unless paid or sent more. Financial sextortion of teenage boys is the fastest-growing variant and most parents have never heard of it. If it is happening: DO NOT PAY (payment increases demands, it does not end them). STOP RESPONDING — have the child cease contact with the perpetrator entirely; do not negotiate, bargain, or send anything further. DO NOT DELETE anything (the evidence is needed) — block only after evidence is preserved. PRESERVE everything — screenshots, usernames, payment demands. REPORT IMMEDIATELY (Cybertip.ca / NCMEC). Reassure the child they are not in trouble and that paying or sending more will not fix it. Speed matters.

### The rehearsed family protocol
- **key:** `rehearsed_protocol`
- **reviewed_by:** DRAFT — pending professional review
- **last_reviewed_at:** 2026-08-19 22:27:02 UTC

Agree as a household, BEFORE it is needed, what to do — and rehearse it. The agreement: if anyone online asks to move to another app, asks for a photo, or asks them to keep a secret from you, they tell you — and they will NEVER be in trouble for telling you. That last part is the point; children stay silent because they fear blame or losing their device, so remove that fear in advance. We do not react in the moment; we rehearse so the child already knows what to do.
